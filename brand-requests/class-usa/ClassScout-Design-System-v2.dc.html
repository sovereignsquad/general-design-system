CLASSSCOUT NYC · DESIGN SYSTEM
A parent planning layer, not a directory
ClassScout helps a parent in New York decide whether a class is worth a call. Most of them are on a phone, one-handed, with a child nearby. The system is built around that moment: warm paper, calm navy, one orange that means act, and a visible confidence layer on every listing so nobody has to guess what is known and what is estimated.

Dropdowns are native
A select is a real select. A styled combobox on a phone puts a caret in the field and raises the keyboard over the options, so choosing one of four things starts by dismissing a keyboard nobody asked for. Native controls are familiar, handle long lists better, and cannot raise a keyboard at all.
Searchable selection for genuinely long lists is the exception and still uses the governed composition, never a bespoke menu.

Rules that bind every badge
Colour comes from a closed set: a semantic tone or one activity accent, never a free value.
Labels are written by a person. "Football", never a glyph's export name.
Under forced colours every fill flattens to one system pair; the label and the stroke are what survive.
Eight accent categories at most on one surface, so a screen stays scannable.
Iconography
Every activity owns its glyph
Outline icons at 2px, one shared registry, no exceptions and no generic fallback mark. Two activities may never share a glyph: a repeated glyph teaches a reader that the glyph means nothing.
Where no literal match exists, depict something real and specific — the equipment, the venue, the apparatus, the posture, the signature prop. Never invent a mark, and never borrow one another activity owns.
Emoji: the map pin, and nowhere else
A pin may carry an emoji for its activity. Cards, thumbnails, hero art, chips, headings, body copy, empty states, buttons and notifications never do.
An activity gets an emoji only where one honestly depicts it; otherwise the pin keeps its Tabler glyph. The emoji is drawn fully opaque, never recoloured, never dimmed, and always hidden from assistive technology — the pin's written name carries the meaning.
Emoji rendering varies by device, so support is measured rather than assumed and the glyph is the fallback whenever it is missing.
Imagery
Generated from the listing's own activities
ClassScout shows no photographs of listings. Artwork is composed from the listing's own data, seeded by its id, so a card is complete in the first response with nothing to fetch and nothing to fail. A card never depends on a network image to be finished.
Card thumbnail
Soccer
A pastel ground — the accent at 18% into cream — with the activity's own glyph oversized to overflow two edges at 18% opacity. Angle, scale and position are seeded from the listing id, so a card composes once and keeps that composition forever.
Two layers, deliberately separate
The ground is decorative texture and may crop freely — losing an edge costs nothing.
The badges are real controls laid over it: up to three, bottom-left, the lead one named and the rest icon-only. Each filters the search by that activity and carries its own accessible name.
Badges are fully saturated because a ground at 18% cannot carry colour coding. Soft ground, saturated small elements — that relationship holds everywhere in the system.
Where a tile is too small for a pill to be legible, badges are suppressed and the surrounding copy names the activity instead.

Basemap and neighbourhoods
The canvas and a wash over the basemap are both cream. Desaturation alone yields a grey map; the cream composite is what makes it read as ours.
Neighbourhood fills are categorical, not a measurement — the fill says which neighbourhood you are looking at and nothing more. Each is an accent at 42% into cream, painted at 55% opacity so streets stay legible, with a paper hairline between them.
Adjacent neighbourhoods never share a family, so two bordering areas cannot read as one region. Areas outside coverage take a faded paper wash.
Map pixels are not in the accessibility tree on any library. Anything that exists only on the map is unreachable for some readers, so the synced list beside it carries every result.

What the preview must answer
Age, place, cost, source and freshness — the same contract every listing surface carries. A preview with no cost and no last-checked date asks the parent to tap through before they can judge anything.
The summary is one written sentence, 100 to 160 characters, in the house shape: format, age or interest, context, benefit. Never a provider's marketing text cut mid-word.
Save sits beside the primary action, because saving and opening are both jobs a parent has here.

Overlays
Modals are for confirmation, a focused edit, or a blocking decision. Focus is trapped, modals never stack, and on a phone they run near full width.
Drawers and bottom sheets carry filters and secondary panels, with explicit apply, reset and close.
Destructive confirmations restate the target by name: "Remove Noah's profile?", never "Are you sure?".

Breakpoints and grids
Cards step one column on a phone, two on a tablet, three on desktop, halving in split map and list mode.
Mobile is for finding fast, tablet for comparing, desktop for planning. The trust logic and hierarchy are identical everywhere; only density adapts.
Rotation and back-navigation preserve filters, scroll, the selected listing and the map bounds. A search is never reset by moving.
Page headers
A header answers three questions: where am I, what is this for, what can I do next. The page's primary action lives there.
Breadcrumbs on desktop detail pages; "Back to results" on mobile. View details and Save are never hidden in an overflow menu.
Tables and lists
A desktop table compressed onto a phone is not acceptable. Choose one: horizontal scroll, a card view, priority columns, or stacked rows.
A list card shows one visible primary action; the rest move to overflow. Adjacent icon-only clusters invite mis-taps and are avoided.
Filters sit next to the data they affect, and the toolbar order is stable: search, filter, sort, reset, create.

How Scout speaks
It offers, it does not promise: "activities that may fit", always closing with confirmation by the provider. It never claims to know every class, and never says a listing is safe or guaranteed.
Beta is labelled and explained. The privacy line sits next to the input, not buried in settings.
Quick starts are parent-intent labels — "This weekend", "Free & low-cost" — composed from live categories, so a switched-off category never appears as a suggestion.
The navy sheet is the one dark surface in the system, and only because Scout is a distinct mode. Nothing else inverts.
Words
Voice
Calm, practical, local, adult to adult. Decision support, not marketing. Second person and direct: "Find trusted kids' activities across NYC."
One brand name everywhere: ClassScout NYC, or ClassScout in tight space. Never a variant.
Uncertainty is visible
Prices carry "estimated from" and "confirm with provider". Listings show their source and the date they were last checked. Past the freshness window they say details may have changed.
Unknowns are stated — "Age not confirmed", "Price unknown" — never quietly omitted.
Words we don't use
Guaranteed, safe, perfect match, best, trust us. "Verified" appears only as the defined provider badge.
Never an invented name for a concept with a standard one, and never an internal identifier on screen.
Composed, not hardcoded
A sentence never names a category, activity or neighbourhood that has been switched off, and degrades as its list shrinks to two, to one, to none.
Every slot that holds text must have an answer for the day there is nothing true to say in it.
